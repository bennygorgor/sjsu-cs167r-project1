How Fast Does Normal Happen?

Five populations, 30,000 repetitions per experiment, and a test of the n = 30 rule

1 Executive summary

This investigation uses R Monte Carlo simulations to examine the sampling distribution of the sample mean, rather than whether the original population becomes normal. Under the declared approximation criteria and primary seed 5533, the selected sample sizes are 1 for the normal population, 6 for the die, 51 for the exponential population, and 63 for Binomial(5, 0.10). No n works for the standard Cauchy mean.

These are empirical, criterion-dependent selections, not universal mathematical cutoffs. In particular, the alternate-seed exponential run selects 39 instead of 51. The robust conclusion is that the population’s shape, discreteness, tail behavior, and the accuracy one needs all matter; 30 is not a universal threshold.

3 Simulation and interpretation

For every configuration, B=30,000 independent samples are generated and each contains n observations. The code resets the seed to 5533 immediately before each primary experiment. Independent observations within a sample and independent repetitions are simulated in all five cases. Larger B reduces Monte Carlo uncertainty; larger n changes the distribution of the mean. These roles are not interchangeable.

For finite-variance populations, the initial n grid is 1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200. All integers up to the first coarse passing value plus four are then evaluated, extending if needed. Cauchy uses n=1, 2, 5, 10, 30, 100, 300, 1000.

The numerical rule requires absolute skewness ≤0.30, absolute excess kurtosis ≤0.50, maximum CDF distance from the theoretical normal ≤0.05, and relative empirical-SE error ≤5%. The first n beginning five consecutive passing integers is the candidate. The histograms and Q–Q plots support using it for central shape and moderate global probability accuracy, with remaining tail and discreteness limitations explicitly discussed. These are practical tolerances rather than significance tests. A complete definition appears in each analysis.

5 Does n = 30 work?

population n skewness excess_kurtosis cdf_distance pass
normal 30 0.0011 -0.0131 0.0069 TRUE
die 30 0.0177 -0.0585 0.0242 TRUE
exponential 30 0.3781 0.2664 0.0291 FALSE
binomial 30 0.2243 0.0928 0.0704 FALSE

Normal and die pass at 30. Exponential fails because of residual skewness. Small binomial fails the CDF discrepancy criterion even though its skewness is below 0.30. Cauchy does not satisfy the finite-variance premise at all. Thus neither “30 is always necessary” nor “30 is always sufficient” is supported.

6 Why convergence differs

Normal sample means are exactly normal. For the die, population symmetry removes skewness from the outset, while averaging smooths a bounded lattice distribution. Exponential means have theoretical skewness \(2/\sqrt n\), so noticeable asymmetry can remain at moderately large n. Binomial means also have decreasing skewness, but probability jumps remain visible to an uncorrected CDF comparison. Under this combined rule, binomial selects the largest finite n; under a skewness-only rule the ranking could change.

Cauchy is qualitatively different. Its average has exactly the original Cauchy distribution, so no increase in n produces concentration around a finite mean. A symmetric central histogram is not sufficient evidence for the ordinary CLT.

7 Reproducibility and uncertainty

The second-seed exponential experiment repeats the full selection procedure using 5534. It selects 39, versus 51 with 5533. Both reject n=30 under the same criteria. The exact selected integer changes; the conclusion about slow improvement of a finite-variance right-skewed population does not.

For a normal distribution, the rough Monte Carlo standard errors of skewness and excess kurtosis at B=30,000 are \(\sqrt{6/B}\approx0.0141\) and \(\sqrt{24/B}\approx0.0283\). These are reference scales, not exact uncertainty formulas for every population. Near a tolerance boundary, small diagnostic changes can move the selected n substantially. The five-pass rule cannot eliminate this issue, especially with correlated configurations from reusing a seed.

The die’s CDF discrepancy at n=6 is 0.049965, barely below 0.05. This is a borderline practical selection, not a robust guarantee. More repetitions, additional prespecified seeds, or stricter tolerances would be appropriate for a high-accuracy application. The finite search and diagnostic thresholds also do not promise accuracy for extreme-tail probabilities.

8 CLT assumptions and limits

The ordinary iid CLT applies to the four finite-variance populations in this project: observations are independent, identically distributed, and have finite variance. Its standard-error prediction is \(\sigma/\sqrt n\). The simulations compare this prediction with the SD of the generated sample means.

Cauchy violates the finite-variance requirement. Dependence and non-identical distributions were removed from the required simulations by the update, so no empirical conclusions about those optional processes are claimed. In general, dependence adds covariance terms to the variance of a mean, while non-identical observations may require a generalized CLT and a different variance calculation. They cannot automatically inherit the ordinary iid formula.
